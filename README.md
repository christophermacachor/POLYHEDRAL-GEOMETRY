## Abstract

This research examines polyhedral geometry as a structural representation for investigating planetary form, solar reception, and cross-scale geometric relationships. A polyhedron retains discrete planar faces, edges, vertices, face areas, and face-normal directions. These geometric properties provide measurable quantities for examining orientation, projected area, directional exposure, and structural differentiation.

The planetary research program focuses on the June solstice and Arctic regions of North America, Greenland, and northern Eurasia. Locations north of the Arctic Circle experience continuous daylight during the relevant seasonal interval. Daylight duration, solar declination, geographic latitude, Earth–Sun distance, incident irradiance, and regional temperature are treated as distinct variables. Temperature comparisons require identified stations, defined climatological periods, and traceable observational sources.

The mathematical program includes face-resolved solar incidence and projected-area calculations. For a planar face i, the idealized direct-beam contribution may be expressed as P_i = E(r) A_i max(0, n_i · s), where E(r) is solar irradiance at the relevant Earth–Sun distance, A_i is the face area, n_i is the outward unit normal, and s is the unit vector toward the incoming source. This equation describes geometric projection; it does not establish that Earth is physically a regular polyhedron.

The cross-scale research direction investigates whether polyhedral descriptors—faces, edges, vertices, adjacency, orientation, and scale-dependent measurements—can serve as a comparative structural language across atomic, molecular, biological, and planetary examples. Similarity of mathematical description is not, by itself, evidence of a shared physical mechanism.

The work follows the Macachor territory–cartography distinction: territory is reality; cartography is representation; correspondence is the test. Models are representations whose validity must be assessed through mathematical consistency, correspondence with observations, explicit uncertainty, and falsifiable comparisons.

This record presents a research framework and model-development program. It distinguishes observed measurements, derived geometric quantities, proposed interpretations, and unresolved empirical questions. It does not claim that a polyhedral Earth geometry has been established by the evidence presented here.

## Research scope

1. **Polyhedral geometry:** compare selectable polyhedral families using explicit face, edge, vertex, normal-vector, area, and adjacency descriptors.
2. **Planetary solar geometry:** calculate solar declination, local hour angle, latitude-dependent daylight duration, Earth–Sun distance scaling, face incidence, and projected area.
3. **Arctic observational comparison:** document 24-hour daylight conditions north of the Arctic Circle around the June solstice and compare regional temperatures only where station identity, averaging period, units, and authoritative source are recorded.
4. **Cross-scale comparison:** investigate whether geometric descriptors can be meaningfully compared across atomic, molecular, biological, and planetary examples without assuming that geometric analogy proves common physical ontology.
5. **Empirical adjudication:** compare predictions against observations and conventional reference models; report uncertainty, assumptions, and possible falsifiers.

## Core geometric expression

For a planar face *i* under direct illumination:

`P_i = E(r) A_i max(0, n_i · s)`

where:

- `P_i` is the idealized incident power on the face;
- `E(r)` is direct-beam irradiance at Earth–Sun distance `r`;
- `A_i` is the face area;
- `n_i` is the outward unit face-normal vector;
- `s` is the unit vector toward the incoming source.

This is a projected-area relationship. A complete planetary energy budget must additionally account for the actual planetary boundary geometry, atmospheric transmission and scattering, clouds, albedo, surface properties, rotation, orbital geometry, and spatial integration. The face-resolved equation alone does not establish a polyhedral physical Earth.

## Arctic summer-solstice treatment

The June-solstice analysis must distinguish:

- axial tilt and solar declination;
- geographic latitude and local hour angle;
- polar-day conditions and local solar incidence;
- Earth–Sun distance and irradiance scaling;
- observed temperature and its multiple climatic controls.

The Arctic Circle is approximately 66.56° N under the present obliquity. Continuous daylight at higher latitudes near the June solstice follows from the Sun's daily path remaining above the local horizon. It should not be attributed to a planar face or to Earth being closer to the Sun. Earth reaches perihelion in early January and aphelion in early July; the solstice and aphelion are distinct orbital events.

Temperature tables must not be populated with unsupported illustrative numbers. Each station record should include station name, country/region, coordinates, elevation where available, dataset/provider, climatological normal period, month, statistic, units, retrieval date, and a persistent citation or URL.
